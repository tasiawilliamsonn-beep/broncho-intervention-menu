Why this menu exists

Three problems we are solving

Our discipline data and staff conversations show three patterns. Each one has a fix that is built into this site.

1

Consequences without intervention

Students receive detentions, lost privileges, or referrals before anyone has taught, prompted, or reinforced the expected behavior.

The fix: the Intervention Menu shows only proactive and instructional Tier 1 interventions, and clearly labels consequences as not interventions.

2

"I've tried everything," with no record

Without documented interventions, dates, and student response, a support team cannot tell what was tried, for how long, or whether it worked.

The fix: the Log Builder writes the intervention into every entry and keeps a running Proof of Intervention record for each student on your device.

3

Mild behaviors logged as Discipline

Level 1 and Level 2 behaviors are handled in the classroom. Logging them as DISCIPLINE sends them to administrators before classroom supports have been used.

The fix: the Log Builder reads the matrix level of what you saw and assigns the log type: INTERVENTION, INFORMATION, COMMUNICATION, or DISCIPLINE.

What is Tier 1?

The classroom teacher is the first responder

In a Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS), Tier 1 is the foundation: high-quality, research-based instruction and behavior support that every student receives in every classroom. When Tier 1 is strong, about 80% of students are successful without extra help. Students move to Tier 2 or Tier 3 only after well-delivered, documented Tier 1 interventions show the student needs more.

Tier 1 behavior support has three layers:

  • School-wide expectations. HEART is defined, taught in every setting, and acknowledged consistently.
  • Class-wide management. Taught routines, engaging instruction, active supervision, high rates of praise, and strong relationships.
  • Classroom individual interventions. When one student struggles, the teacher chooses an evidence-based intervention, records it, and tracks progress.

Sources: Wright, Intervention Central (ONC BOCES MTSS Tier 1 training, 2023); RTI Handbook tier definitions; Sugai & Horner, 2002.

The most important distinction

An intervention is not a consequence

A consequence is something that happens after a behavior. An intervention is a planned action that changes the conditions or builds the skill so the behavior is less likely to happen again. Only interventions count as Tier 1 evidence.

Intervention Teaches or prevents

A proactive or instructional strategy, matched to why the behavior is happening, used consistently for long enough to judge whether it works.

  • Re-teaching and practicing the expectation
  • Pre-correction before a hard transition
  • Behavior-specific praise for the replacement behavior
  • Chunking work the student is avoiding
  • A break card, a self-monitoring checklist, strategic seating
  • A re-entry or restorative conversation
VS

Consequence Responds after the fact

An outcome imposed after the behavior. Some consequences are appropriate and listed on our matrix, but by themselves they teach no new skill and are not intervention.

  • Lunch or after-school detention
  • Loss of privilege or recess
  • Restitution or a forced apology letter
  • Removal from class or office time-out
  • A phone call home only to report the behavior
  • Office referral, suspension

Research rule of thumb: use negative consequences sparingly, and only after you have (a) tried supportive responses and (b) ruled out causes the student cannot control, such as a skill deficit (Conroy & Sutherland, 2012). Positive consequences such as specific praise do count as intervention because they strengthen the expected behavior.

Behavior happens for a reason

Match the intervention to the function

Problem behavior serves a purpose for the student. When you can name the likely purpose (its function), you can choose an intervention that meets the same need in an acceptable way. The menu uses these six functions to rank interventions.

Peer attention

Classmates laugh, react, or join in. Try the Good Behavior Game, tootling, strategic seating, and praise for peers.

Adult attention

The behavior reliably gets the teacher's attention. Try pre-session attention, scheduled attention, planned ignoring with praise for the positive.

Escape or avoidance

The behavior gets the student out of a task or situation. Check instructional match, chunk the work, and teach a break card. Removal from class can reward this.

Access to items or activities

The student wants a device, object, or preferred activity. Try "no" with a preferred alternative and first/then statements.

Skill deficit

The student has not learned the behavior or cannot do the work yet. Teach it explicitly, practice it, and use checklists.

Emotional, attentional, or physical need

Frustration, anxiety, fatigue, or attention needs get in the way. Try a calm, brief response, scheduled breaks, and self-monitoring.

Function categories: Packenham, Shute, & Reid, 2004; Gable et al., 2009. Use an ABC statement (Antecedent, Behavior, Consequence) to find the pattern for chronic behaviors.

Building a strong plan

Four kinds of Tier 1 interventions

A strong classroom plan combines strategies that prevent the behavior, teach the replacement, reinforce it, and respond in the moment without rewarding the problem behavior (Wright, 2018).

PreventChange the setting before behavior happens: precorrection, seating, choice, chunking, greetings.
TeachBuild the missing skill: model, practice, replacement behaviors, self-monitoring.
ReinforceMake the expected behavior pay off: specific praise, 4:1 ratio, Good Behavior Game.
RespondRedirect calmly and briefly: remind-redirect-reinforce, Stop-Start-Praise, re-entry conference.
The Broncho process

Five steps before any referral

  1. Name the behavior and its level. Describe exactly what you saw (observable, measurable) and find it on the matrix.
  2. Hypothesize the function. What does the behavior get the student? When does it happen?
  3. Choose 2 or 3 matched interventions. Include at least one Prevent or Teach strategy, not only Respond.
  4. Implement and log. Use the intervention consistently and log each use as INTERVENTION. For Level 2, contact the family as a partner and log it as COMMUNICATION.
  5. Review the data after 2 to 3 weeks. Is it working? Continue, adjust, or bring your documented record to the MTSS/RTI team.

Progress monitoring every 2 to 3 weeks and team review follow the RTI Handbook Tier 1 process.

Minimum Tier 1 expectations

What "tried everything" must show

  • The behavior is described clearly and specifically.
  • Evidence-based intervention strategies were used.
  • Instructional adjustments or accommodations were made as appropriate.
  • Intervention efforts were recorded (written down, with dates).
  • Data was collected on whether behavior or engagement improved.
  • The teacher communicated with the student.
  • The teacher communicated with the parent(s). For a Level 3 "no change" referral, the matrix requires at least three logged communications.

Adapted from "What are your expectations of the teacher as behavioral first responder?" (Wright, ONC BOCES Tier 1 training, 2023).

PowerTeacher log instructions

Choose the right log type

INTERVENTION

Level 1 or 2 behavior you have addressed. Always include the intervention you used and how the student responded.

INFORMATION

Details you want documented (a pattern you noticed, a student's report, a schedule change).

COMMUNICATION

Written or verbal contact with a parent or guardian about the student. Required for Level 2.

DISCIPLINE

Only Level 3 and Level 4 behaviors that you want an administrator to address. Not for Level 1 or 2 behaviors.
