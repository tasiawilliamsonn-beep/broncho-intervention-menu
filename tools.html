Essential Academic & Social Behaviors

In Behavior Solutions, Hannigan, Djabrayan Hannigan, Mattos, and Buffum (2020) define behavior as two kinds of skills: academic behaviors (skill-based) and social behaviors (will-based). Both must be taught and reinforced at every tier, starting with Tier 1 prevention. Tier 1 begins by identifying the essential behaviors every student must learn and show, then teaching them like academic standards.

  1. Rate each behavior below for its impact on learning and how many students struggle with it. Do this as a team if you can.
  2. Focus on your top 3 academic and top 3 social behaviors. Print them for your room or team.
  3. Plan a lesson for each one and teach it like content: model, example and non-example, practice, feedback, and reinforcement. Re-teach after breaks.

The Broncho Shows You How

Students learn behavior the way they learn academics: by seeing it modeled, practicing, and getting feedback (Center on PBIS, 2022; Fairbanks et al., 2007). Use these animated Broncho posters to model each expectation, then have students act it out. Print one at a time or all eight on one page.

Student Reminder Posters

Visual prompts for routines students use every day. Teach each routine first, then point to the poster as a quick prompt instead of a verbal reminder (Kern & Clemens, 2007).

Free Incentives

Recognition makes expected behavior worth repeating. Free rewards work as well as purchased ones when they are tied to a specific HEART behavior, given right away, paired with praise, and chosen from a menu. Start rich, then fade to praise and occasional surprises.

Make it work: name the HEART behavior every time you give a Broncho Buck ("You started right away. That's Effort."). Make rewards earnable by every student. Never take away a Buck that was already earned. Rotate the menu so it stays motivating, and gradually give rewards less often as the behavior becomes a habit (Kazdin, 2013).

Using Broncho Bucks

Broncho Bucks are Sunnyside's school-wide reward, so use the Broncho Bucks Sunnyside already provides. They work best when every Buck is paired with specific praise for a HEART behavior. The Daily Behavior Report on this page can set a daily Broncho Bucks goal for an individual student.

Class Jobs That Build Belonging

Class jobs give every student a real role, a reason to move, and a positive way to get attention. Evidence note: we did not find rigorous studies of classroom jobs alone. This structure is based on related research: teacher support and positive roles predict school belonging (Allen et al., 2018), and matching supports to why a behavior happens works better than one-size-fits-all (Gable et al., 2009).

How to run class jobs

  1. Give real responsibility. Sixth graders care about being respected and trusted. Use grown-up titles, let students apply for jobs, and let them do the job without being micromanaged (Yeager, Dahl, & Dweck, 2018).
  2. Every student has a job. Belonging means everyone contributes. Share a job between two students if needed.
  3. Teach each job like a routine. Model it, practice it, and post the job card with its steps.
  4. Rotate weekly. Change jobs every Monday and log it, so every student gets every type of role.
  5. Match jobs on purpose. A student who seeks attention may thrive as Discussion Leader or Brain Break Leader. A student who needs movement can be Messenger or Materials Manager. A student who struggles with peers may grow as Welcome Ambassador. These are healthy ways to meet the same need.
  6. Praise the job, not just the student. "Thanks for having the materials ready before the bell. That's Teamwork."
  7. Never remove a job as punishment. The job is part of the support plan. If a job isn't going well, re-teach it.

Behavior Contracts

A meta-analysis of 18 studies found behavior contracts had moderate positive effects on behavior, along with benefits for academic outcomes (Bowman-Perrott et al., 2015). A good contract is positive (what the student will do), specific (observable), short (1 to 2 weeks), achievable (start where the student can succeed), and shared (the teacher commits too). Start with a whole-class agreement; use an individual contract for a student who needs more.

Visiting Pass (Buddy Classroom)

A buddy classroom is a planned, short reset in a partner teacher's room. It is a calm-down support, not a punishment or a removal to the office. Evidence note: buddy rooms come from practitioner guidance (Responsive Classroom, n.d.), and we did not find studies of buddy rooms specifically. The rules below apply research on classroom removals, re-entry, and escape-motivated behavior.

Rules for using it well

  • Plan it ahead. Agree with a partner teacher, ideally in a nearby room with a different grade or class.
  • Teach the routine first. Students practice going, sitting in the reset spot, and returning before they ever need it.
  • Keep it short. 10 minutes or less, with a timer. The student brings work or the reflection sheet.
  • Keep it calm. The receiving teacher welcomes the student quietly and does not lecture.
  • Hold a re-entry conference. Reconnect, talk about the trigger, and agree on a plan (Fields, 2004).
  • Document every visit. Log the time out and the time back as an INTERVENTION entry (Wright, 2018).
  • Watch for escape. If a student asks to go mostly to avoid work, check the work level, chunk the task, or use a break card in your room instead (Waller & Higbee, 2010).
  • Not for Level 3 or 4. Unsafe behavior or an office referral goes to an administrator, not a buddy room.

Daily Behavior Report

A Daily Behavior Report is a quick, reliable way to show whether an intervention is working (Chafouleas, Riley-Tillman, & Sugai, 2007). Mark each behavior with a ✓ (the student showed it) or an ✗ (not yet) at the same time each day for 2 to 4 weeks. To keep marks consistent, decide in advance what earns a ✓: the student did it about as well as a typical classmate.
